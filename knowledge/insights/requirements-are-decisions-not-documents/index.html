Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents

Requirements fail after sign-off because a document records what was agreed, not why. The RDR Model reframes them as durable decision records that cut rework.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 7 min read Intermediate Business Analysis & Requirements #requirements #decision records #business analysis #traceability #governance #RDR Model

The gap where rework begins

Enterprise programs rarely fail because teams cannot write requirements. They fail because organizations make consequential decisions and preserve none of the reasoning behind them. Months pass, the people rotate, the context fades, and the documents remain, authoritative and hollow. That gap between a decision and its record is where expensive rework begins, and it almost always announces itself late.

It looks like this. A transformation program is six weeks from go-live. User acceptance testing surfaces that a core process was built exactly to the letter of the specification, and it is wrong. The sponsor is certain the team was told what the business needed. The delivery lead points to a signed document. The architect explains that the design followed the requirement as written. Everyone is telling the truth, and the program is still stuck.

If you have led enterprise delivery, you recognize the symptoms long before this moment: budgets that overrun for reasons no one can name, scope that keeps moving while everyone insists it is fixed, stakeholders who disagree about what was agreed, and acceptance testing that reveals the wrong solution built correctly. The reflex is to blame the requirements document, tighten the template, and add another approval gate. The document is not the problem. The belief that a document was ever the point is the problem.

Why the document mindset fails

The dominant model treats requirements as an artifact to gather, sign, and shelve. It fails for structural reasons, so better templating never fixes it.

A signed requirement records the what. It rarely records the why, almost never records who is accountable for the choice, and has nowhere to hold the assumptions the choice depends on. When one of those assumptions quietly breaks months later, nothing flags it, because the reasoning was never captured.

Requirements are also frozen at the moment of least knowledge. Sign-off usually happens near the start of a program, when the team understands the problem least, and the document is then treated as settled exactly as understanding improves. That inverts the natural order of learning.

Finally, the document makes change adversarial. Because the baseline is a signed contract, every adjustment becomes a change request against the spec, to be resisted or negotiated rather than reasoned through. The artifact is optimized for approval, not for the decisions it is meant to carry, and a signature is a poor substitute for a rationale.

The reframe: requirements carry decisions

If the document is the wrong container, what is the right one? Read a real requirement closely and you will find a decision inside it: to configure or to customize, to centralize or to federate, to automate a step or leave it manual, to accept a risk or to mitigate it. The sentence in the document is only the visible residue of that choice. When the choice is sound and its reasoning is captured, the requirement holds. When the choice was never really made, or its reasoning was never recorded, the requirement fails the first time reality tests it.

Key insight

A requirement is not the decision. It is the documented outcome of a business decision. Capture the decision, with its reasoning, and the requirement takes care of itself.

This is not a business-analysis nicety; it is an enterprise decision-making model. The executive who needs governance, the architect who needs design rationale, the program manager who needs disciplined change control, and the analyst who needs traceability are all asking for the same underlying thing: the decision, and the reasoning behind it, kept somewhere durable. Treat requirements as decisions and one record serves all four.

Applying it in an enterprise program

The point is not to document more. It is to document the right things and make reasoning first-class. In practice that means five moves.

First, separate the significant requirements from the routine. Most are uncontested and cheap to reverse; leave them as ordinary statements. Reserve the decision lens for choices that are contested, expensive, hard to reverse, or regulated. The discipline is in the few, not the many.

Second, for each significant requirement, capture the decision behind it rather than only the statement of it. “The system shall calculate withholding tax per local rules” hides a real choice about configuration versus customization. Record the choice.

Third, give every such decision an owner. A decision without an accountable owner is a wish, and wishes are what people argue about later.

Fourth, record assumptions as first-class and wire them to a revisit trigger. Requirements rarely fail because they were wrong on day one; they fail because an assumption they quietly depended on stopped being true and no one was watching.

Fifth, connect the decision forward, so traceability follows reasoning rather than paperwork:

  1. 1

    Business Problem

  2. 2

    Decision

    Owner · Rationale · Assumptions

  3. 3

    Requirement

  4. 4

    Acceptance Criteria

  5. 5

    Tested Business Outcome

The same chain gives governance its audit trail, gives delivery its basis for change control, and gives the business a straight line from the problem it cared about to the outcome it can verify.

The framework: the RDR Model

To make this repeatable without recreating the bloated document it replaces, attach a short Requirements Decision Record to each significant requirement. We call this the RDR Model. It answers five questions and fits on a single screen.

FieldThe question it answersWhy it matters
DecisionWhat is actually being decided?Names the choice hiding inside the requirement.
RationaleWhy is this the chosen option?Preserves the reasoning a signature discards.
OwnerWho is accountable for this decision?Turns a wish into an owned commitment.
AssumptionsWhat must stay true for this to hold?Makes the failure conditions visible in advance.
Revisit whenWhat event or date reopens it?Converts silent drift into a deliberate review.

The RDR Model is deliberately lightweight. It is not a deliverable to maintain for its own sake; it is the connective tissue between the business case and the acceptance criteria for the decisions that matter. Used well, it changes the analyst’s role from scribe to decision facilitator, which is the point.

A realistic example

Consider a finance transformation for a multi-entity organization that must handle a country-specific withholding tax rule. As a document, the requirement reads: “The system shall calculate withholding tax per local rules.” Signed, filed, done.

As an RDR Model record, it reads:

  • Decision: Configure the platform’s standard tax engine rather than build custom logic.
  • Rationale: Preserve upgradeability and lower total cost of ownership; the standard engine covers the current rule set.
  • Owner: Finance transformation lead, with the solution architect accountable for feasibility.
  • Assumptions: The jurisdiction’s rules stay within the standard engine’s parameters, and no bespoke rounding rule is mandated.
  • Revisit when: A jurisdiction introduces a rule the standard engine cannot express, or a new entity is onboarded.

Months later, a newly acquired entity needs a bespoke rounding rule. In the document world, this becomes a dispute: the spec was signed, so the change is contested, escalated, and expensive to unpick. With the RDR Model, an assumption’s trigger simply fired. The team reopens the recorded decision, sees exactly why “configure” was chosen and precisely what would change it, and makes the “customize” call deliberately, with a clean audit trail. The argument never happens, because the reasoning was never lost.

Where this works, and where it does not

The RDR Model earns its keep on high-stakes, contested, long-lived, regulated, or multi-stakeholder programs, exactly the ERP, CRM, integration, and platform-selection work where a forgotten or unowned decision is expensive to recover from.

It is the wrong tool for small teams, short-lived work, or well-understood domains with little uncertainty, where decision records add ceremony without payoff. The most common failure is over-application: put a record on every requirement and you have rebuilt the heavyweight document you were trying to escape. The skill is choosing the vital few decisions worth the rigor.

The trade-off is real. The model front-loads a little discipline, naming owners and stating assumptions before delivery pressure arrives, and some cultures resist that as bureaucracy. The return is fewer late disputes, deliberate rather than adversarial change, and audit-readiness that arrives as a byproduct instead of a separate project. On the programs where it fits, that trade is heavily in your favor.

Key takeaways

  • Requirements fail after sign-off because a document records the what, not the why, the who, or the when to revisit.
  • A requirement is the documented outcome of a decision; capture the decision and the requirement follows.
  • The RDR Model records five things for each significant requirement: the decision, the rationale, the owner, the assumptions, and the revisit trigger.
  • Apply it to the vital few decisions, not to every requirement.
  • Wire the chain from business problem to decision to requirement to acceptance criteria to tested outcome, so traceability follows reasoning.
  • The payoff is fewer disputes, deliberate change control, and audit-readiness without extra effort.

Where to go next

The RDR Model becomes real practice through a deliberate progression, not a pile of links.

  • Requirements Decision Record template - apply the model immediately, with a fillable, one-screen record your teams can adopt this week.
  • Requirements Decision Record framework - see how the RDR Model connects to enterprise decision governance, so it strengthens your operating model rather than sitting beside it.
  • Requirements sign-off readiness checklist - validate that the decisions behind a release are owned, evidenced, and safe before the approval gate.
  • Requirements elicitation playbook - improve how these decisions are surfaced in discovery, so you record them in the room instead of finding them in testing.

Followed in order, that path takes a team from grasping the idea to running it as a standard.
