Assurance Is What Lets Leaders Say Yes

Governance that only says no is cost. Proportionate Assurance scales scrutiny to consequence, so leaders approve fast with evidence behind them.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 7 min read Intermediate Governance, Risk & Assurance #governance #assurance #risk management #audit readiness #decision-making

The gate everyone routes around

A delivery team needs approval to release a change. The governance process requires a completed checklist, two review boards, and a risk assessment written in a template nobody reads. The team knows the change is low risk; the process cannot tell the difference, so it charges everyone the same toll. Three weeks later the change is approved, unaltered, by people who added no scrutiny anyone could name. Meanwhile, in the same organization, a genuinely dangerous change slides through on the same checklist, ticked with the same speed, because the process that inspects everything examines nothing.

Then the sponsor asks the question governance exists to answer: “Can I approve this with confidence?” And the honest reply, after all that process, is: nobody actually knows. The friction was real. The assurance was not.

This is governance’s reputation problem, and it is earned. Teams experience it as toll collection, leaders experience it as paperwork that arrives with a recommendation already attached, and both quietly route around it: teams by batching and reframing changes to dodge the gates, leaders by approving on relationships instead of evidence. The controls remain. The control is gone.

Why current thinking fails

Standard governance fails structurally, in three ways.

It is uniform where risk is not. One process, one checklist, one gate, applied identically to the trivial and the dangerous. Uniformity feels rigorous and is the opposite: when everything gets the same scrutiny, scrutiny becomes a formality, because no reviewer can afford real attention on everything and so gives it to nothing.

It produces compliance, not confidence. The artifacts prove the process was followed, not that the risky thing is under control. A completed template answers “did we do governance?” It does not answer the sponsor’s actual question, which is “what could go wrong here, and what evidence says it is handled?”

And it defines success as prevention, which makes governance the department of no. A function whose only visible output is friction will be routed around, and routed-around governance is worse than none, because it leaves everyone believing a control exists where none is operating.

The reframe: assurance is an enabling function

Governance is not there to slow things down or to catch people out. It exists so that a leader can say yes with confidence, because someone can show, with evidence, that the risky thing is under control. That is assurance, and it is an enabling function: its product is confident decisions, made faster, on evidence.

Key insight

Governance that cannot enable a confident yes is just cost. The test of an assurance regime is not how much it inspects but whether leaders can approve quickly, with evidence behind them.

Held to that test, the design question changes. Not “what must every change pass through?” but “how much scrutiny does this decision deserve, and what evidence would let its approver say yes without flinching?” Scrutiny becomes something you spend, deliberately, where it buys confidence, instead of a tax you levy evenly and collect in resentment.

The framework: the Proportionate Assurance model

The Proportionate Assurance model scales assurance effort to two properties of the thing being approved: its consequence (what a failure costs) and its reversibility (how cheaply a failure can be undone). Those two properties, not the org chart and not the template, determine which of three assurance tiers applies.

TierWhen it appliesEvidence requiredWho verifies
Self-attestationLow consequence, easily reversibleThe owner attests against a short standard; evidence retained, not reviewedThe accountable owner
Peer reviewMaterial consequence, or awkward to reverseA qualified peer examines the evidence and challenges the approachSomeone independent of the work, inside the domain
Independent verificationHigh consequence, hard to reverse, or regulatedEvidence is tested, not just read: sampled, re-performed, tracedA function independent of the delivery chain

Two rules make the model work. First, the tier is decided by consequence and reversibility, never by who is asking or how urgent they claim to be; urgency is precisely when tier discipline earns its keep. Second, every tier produces evidence as a byproduct of doing the work, which is why organizations running proportionate assurance arrive at audits calm: the trail exists because the regime made it, not because a team spent a quarter reconstructing it.

Readers of this series will recognize the design language. A tier assignment is an owned, revisitable decision in the sense of the RDR Model, and the evidence each tier demands is exactly the claim-with-evidence discipline of The Status Report That Tells the Truth, applied at the approval gate instead of the weekly report. Assurance, honestly built, is evidenced status at the moments that matter most.

Why this framework is different

Traditional governance applies the same review process regardless of risk: one checklist, one gate, one cadence for everything. The result is structural, not accidental: unnecessary friction for low-risk work, which pays a toll it does not owe, and insufficient scrutiny for high-risk work, which hides in the same queue and receives the same formality.

The Proportionate Assurance model scales assurance to the decision itself, by consequence, by reversibility, and by the evidence each tier must produce. Scrutiny becomes something spent where it buys confidence rather than levied evenly and collected in resentment.

The goal is not more governance. It is faster decisions with appropriate confidence: low-risk work moving at self-attestation speed, high-risk work earning verification that means something, and leaders approving on evidence proportionate to what the decision could cost.

A realistic example

An enterprise platform program applies the model to three approvals in one week. A configuration change to a report layout: low consequence, reversed in minutes; the owner self-attests against the change standard, and it ships the same day. A new integration touching customer data: material consequence, awkward to reverse; a peer architect reviews the design and the test evidence, challenges the error handling, and approval follows in three days with the review on file. A cutover decision for a finance module in a regulated entity: high consequence, expensive to reverse; independent verification samples the reconciliation evidence, re-performs the critical controls, and the steering committee approves against a verification report rather than a slideware assertion.

Three approvals, three speeds, one regime. The report change was not taxed for the cutover’s risk; the cutover was not waved through on the report change’s checklist. And when the external audit arrives that year, the evidence for all three exists already, in the shape the work produced it.

Where it works, and where it does not

Proportionate assurance earns its keep where decision volume is high and risk is uneven: delivery portfolios, change management at scale, regulated environments where blanket process has calcified. It is disproportionate for small organizations with few consequential decisions, where leaders are close enough to the work to hold the evidence personally. Its honest costs: the tiering judgment must itself be governed, because the predictable failure mode is optimistic tiering, teams arguing consequence downward to reach a cheaper tier; and the model removes a comfort from governance functions, whose value must now show up as confident decisions rather than counted reviews. Both costs are the model working. Tier decisions get owners and revisit triggers, and assurance earns its seat by what it enables.

Key takeaways

  • Uniform governance fails structurally: it taxes the trivial, under-examines the dangerous, and produces compliance instead of confidence.
  • Assurance is an enabling function: its product is a leader’s confident, evidenced yes.
  • The Proportionate Assurance model scales scrutiny by consequence and reversibility across three tiers: self-attestation, peer review, independent verification.
  • Tier by the decision’s properties, never by the asker or the urgency; govern the tiering judgment itself, because optimistic tiering is the failure mode.
  • Every tier produces evidence as a byproduct, so audit-readiness arrives without a fire drill.
  • The payoff is faster approvals and stronger control at the same time.

The point of proportionality

Assurance done well is invisible in both directions. Low-risk work never feels governed, because self-attestation moves at the speed of the work. High-risk work never feels rushed, because the scrutiny it receives is real and everyone can see why it is there. What disappears is the middle condition most organizations live in: universal friction producing universal doubt. Governance earns back its reputation the only way it can, by enabling the yes.

Where to go next

Proportionate assurance becomes practice through the governance progression. The natural next step is the risk register template, which gives consequence and reversibility a working home, followed by the audit-readiness checklist, which tests whether the evidence trail your tiers should be producing actually exists, and the assurance rhythm playbook, which stands the full regime up across a program.
