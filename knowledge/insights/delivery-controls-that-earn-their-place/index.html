Delivery controls that earn their place

Most delivery controls are monitoring with a governance label. A control earns its place only if it can change what leadership decides.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Aug 6, 2026 9 min read Intermediate Project & Delivery Management #governance #delivery #transformation #assurance

The programme with twelve controls and no decisions

A transformation programme two years in has a control environment nobody would call thin. There is a weekly status report, a monthly steering committee, a stage gate at each phase boundary, a RAID log reviewed fortnightly, a benefits tracker, a dependency register, a change control board, and an assurance review scheduled twice a year.

Ask the sponsor what any of it caused last quarter and the answer is uncomfortable. The status report was read. The steering committee met and noted progress. The RAID log grew. The gate was passed, as every gate before it was passed. The benefits tracker recorded a widening variance that nobody was empowered to act on.

The programme is heavily controlled and lightly decided. Every control functions exactly as designed, and the environment as a whole produces almost no decisions.

This is not a reporting failure. The reports may be excellent, and a well-formed status report carrying claims, evidence and a request is a genuine improvement over colour-first reporting, which is the case The Status Report That Tells the Truth makes. But a portfolio of well-formed reports that nobody acts on is a well-documented absence of governance.

The question underneath is one most programmes never ask: which of these controls should exist at all?

Why adding controls makes governance worse

Control environments grow by accretion. Something goes wrong, a control is added, and the addition is proportionate to the incident that prompted it. A missed dependency produces a dependency register. A surprise at a gate produces a pre-gate checkpoint. A benefits shortfall produces a tracker.

Each addition is defensible. The environment as a whole is nobody’s design.

Removal never happens, because removing a control is difficult to defend. The person proposing it is arguing to reduce oversight, and if anything goes wrong afterwards the proposal will be remembered. Nothing in the incentive structure rewards subtraction. So controls accumulate, each one consuming attention that is finite, until the aggregate cost is high and invisible.

The consequence is not merely inefficiency. Attention spent on controls that cannot change anything is attention unavailable for the ones that can. A steering committee working through eleven noting items and one decision has given the decision the attention left over.

Traditional maturity thinking makes this worse by treating control count as evidence of rigour. A programme with more controls scores better against most assessment models than one with fewer, regardless of whether any of them produces a decision.

The reframe: a control is a mechanism that changes what happens

The word control has drifted. In everyday programme use it means anything that observes: a report, a register, a review, a forum. In its original and more useful sense, a control is a mechanism that changes the behaviour of the thing it controls.

A thermostat is a control because it acts. A thermometer is not, however accurate.

Applied to delivery, the distinction is sharp and uncomfortable. A control is a mechanism that can change what leadership does. If no realistic finding from a mechanism would cause anyone to approve differently, redirect differently, or stop, it is not a control. It may be worth keeping, and it may be legally required. But it should be named honestly, because a monitoring instrument counted as a control creates the impression of governance that does not exist.

This reframe has a consequence that most governance advice avoids. If controls are mechanisms that change outcomes, then a programme can improve its governance by removing controls, and most over-controlled programmes will improve it more by subtraction than by any addition available to them.

The framework: the Control Sufficiency Test

The Control Sufficiency Test asks three questions of any control, existing or proposed.

QuestionWhat it establishesWhat it exposes
What decision does this enable?The control has a purpose beyond visibilityControls that exist to be seen to exist
What finding would change that decision?The control can produce a different outcomeControls whose findings are always confirmatory
Who acts, and within what window?The finding reaches someone empoweredControls that produce awareness and no action

The first question is answered with a decision and a decision-maker, both named. Visibility is not a decision. Whether to release the next tranche of funding, by the programme sponsor, is.

The second is answered with a finding, stated concretely. If no realistic output of the control would change the decision, the control confirms rather than informs. That is useful, and it is monitoring.

The third is answered with a person and a timeframe. A finding with no owner produces an agenda item.

The answers sort every control into one of four categories.

Decision-grade. All three answered. The control enables a named decision, could produce a finding that changes it, and reaches someone who can act in time. These earn their place.

Latent. The first two answered, the third not. The control is real, the finding would matter, and nobody is empowered to act on it in time. This is the most dangerous category, because it looks identical to a decision-grade control from the outside. A benefits tracker recording a widening variance that no one can act on is latent, and its existence is worse than its absence, because it satisfies the question are we tracking benefits while doing nothing.

Monitoring. The first answered, the second not. Legitimate, useful, and honest when named as such. A control environment needs instruments. The failure is only in counting them as controls.

Administration. The first unanswered. The control exists because it was added, because it appears on a template, or because removing it would require a conversation nobody wants.

A realistic example

The following is illustrative. It is constructed to show the test applied across a full control portfolio, and no outcomes are claimed.

Take the programme described at the start and run each control through the three questions.

Weekly status report. Decision enabled: none stated. Finding that would change it: unclear, because no decision is attached. This is monitoring, genuinely useful for shared awareness, and honestly named as an instrument rather than a control.

Monthly steering committee. Decision enabled: approve continuation and release funding. Finding that would change it: evidence that a workstream cannot deliver within its envelope. Who acts: the sponsor, at the meeting. Decision-grade, provided the committee actually decides. A committee that receives eleven noting items and approves by default has a decision-grade design and a monitoring practice.

Stage gate. Decision enabled: proceed to the next phase. Finding that would change it: unmet exit criteria. Who acts: the gate chair. Decision-grade by design, and latent in practice if the gate has never been failed. A gate that cannot be failed is a milestone with ceremony.

RAID log. Decision enabled: none identified. Entries are logged, reviewed, and rarely routed anywhere with authority to act. Administration. The concept is sound; this implementation is not a control.

Benefits tracker. Decision enabled: adjust scope where projected benefits fall short. Finding that would change it: a widening variance. Who acts: unclear, because the programme cannot change the business case and the business owner is not in the forum. Latent, and the most consequential finding in the portfolio.

The test’s output is not a score. It is a set of actions: retire the RAID log in its current form or route it to someone with authority; name the status report as monitoring and stop counting it as assurance; fail a gate when the criteria are unmet or stop calling it a gate; and give the benefits tracker an owner who can act on what it reports.

Four controls change, and none of them is added.

The control nobody has

Run the test across a portfolio and a pattern usually appears. Controls cluster around two decisions, continue and change scope, and almost none supports the third.

Most programmes have no stop control.

There is no mechanism whose findings would cause a programme to end, no threshold defined in advance, and no forum whose remit includes termination. The decision remains available in principle and unsupported in practice, which means a programme can only be stopped on someone’s nerve, usually late, usually after the evidence has been available for a long time.

This is worth stating plainly because it is invisible until it matters. A control environment that supports continue and change but not stop is not a control environment; it is a steering mechanism with no brake. Sufficiency is not only about whether individual controls are decision-grade. It is about whether the portfolio covers the decisions leadership actually has to make.

Defining a stop control is uncomfortable and briefly unpopular. It means stating in advance what would make the programme not worth continuing: which conditions, which thresholds, which sustained failure to deliver. That is the same discipline a revisit trigger applies to a decision record, and the reason Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents treats the trigger as constitutive rather than optional. A commitment you cannot deliberately reopen will eventually be reopened by circumstance.

Where it works, and where it does not

The test works best on established programmes with accumulated control environments, which is where the accretion problem is worst and the release of attention is largest.

It is worth least on programmes whose control set was designed rather than accumulated. A methodology that specifies a small, constant set of controls, each attached to a decision, has already done this work at design time. Running the test there confirms what the design intended. Its value is proportional to how much of the control environment arrived by accretion, which in most established programmes is most of it.

It works poorly at mobilisation. Before delivery has shape, the decisions leadership will face are not yet knowable, and screening controls against unknown decisions produces confident answers to unanswerable questions. Run it from the first phase boundary onwards.

Regulatory and statutory controls sit outside it entirely. Where a control is mandated, it exists regardless of the decision it enables. The honest response is to name it as compliance, cost it accurately, and stop implying it provides decision support it was never designed to provide.

It does not resolve the political case. A control may exist because a senior stakeholder needs the reassurance of its existence, and that is a real constraint rather than an analytical error. Naming it as reassurance is more honest than arguing it away, and considerably more honest than counting it as a control.

It sits alongside Proportionate Assurance rather than replacing it. Proportionate Assurance decides how much verification a piece of work earns; this decides which control mechanisms exist to verify anything at all.

And a programme where every control passes has not applied the test. The purpose is to find the ones that do not, and in an accumulated environment there are always several.

Key takeaways

Monitoring and control are different things, and the difference is whether anything changes as a result. A control that cannot change what leadership does is an instrument, and calling it a control creates the impression of governance rather than the substance.

The Control Sufficiency Test asks three questions, what decision, what finding, who acts and by when, and sorts controls into decision-grade, latent, monitoring, and administration. Latent controls are the ones to look for first, because they are indistinguishable from working controls until the moment they are needed.

Sufficiency is a property of the portfolio, not of individual controls. The most common gap is the absence of any control supporting the decision to stop.

Expect the test to produce removals. Governance improves when the attention released from controls that change nothing reaches the controls that can.
