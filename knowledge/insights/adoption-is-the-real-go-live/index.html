Adoption Is the Real Go-Live

Cutover is a technical event; adoption is the business one. The Adoption Ledger tracks the behaviors that prove a transformation actually landed.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 7 min read Intermediate Change Adoption & Stakeholder Management #change adoption #transformation #stakeholder management #benefits realization #operating model

The transformation that dissolved in ninety days

The cutover goes cleanly. The war room stands down, the program team celebrates, and the closing report records a successful go-live. Ninety days later a different picture has formed, quietly. Order entry happens in the new system, but pricing decisions still live in the spreadsheet that was supposed to retire. Approvals flow through the new workflow, except in the two regions where managers approve by email and an assistant keys it in afterward. Usage dashboards look respectable because logging in is not the same as adopting. The old process has reasserted itself under the new system’s skin, and the benefits case, built on the process actually changing, is dissolving in a way no status report was designed to catch.

If you sponsor transformations, you have funded this program. The system was delivered. The transformation was not. And the gap between those two sentences is where a remarkable share of transformation value quietly disappears.

Why current thinking fails

The standard model treats adoption as a workstream of the program: training, communications, and champions, running alongside the build and largely finished by cutover. It fails for three structural reasons.

Adoption is measured by inputs, not outcomes. Training completion, communication reach, and readiness-survey scores all measure what the program did, not what the business now does. Every one of those metrics can be green while the old process survives intact.

Go-live is treated as the finish line, when it is the starting line. The program’s energy, attention, and budget peak at exactly the moment the real contest begins: the ninety days when the workforce decides, one workaround at a time, whether the new way is real. The team that could have intervened is being demobilized while the outcome is still undecided.

And adoption has no ledger. Scope has a register, risks have a register, decisions, if you follow the RDR Model, have a record. Adoption has sentiment. Nobody can say, with evidence, which roles have actually changed how they work, so nobody notices which ones have not until the benefits review makes it unmissable.

The reframe: adoption is the business go-live

Cutover is a technical event: the system is live. Adoption is the business event: the organization works differently. Programs that conflate them declare victory at the technical event and lose the business one by walkover.

Key insight

Go-live proves the system works. Adoption proves the organization changed. Only the second one pays for the program, and it must be managed like an outcome, with evidence, not like a mood.

Treated this way, adoption stops being a communications activity and becomes a managed outcome with the same discipline delivery already applies to scope: defined, observable, owned, and tracked until done. The question changes from “how do people feel about the change?” to “which behaviors, in which roles, are now demonstrably different?”, and that question has the useful property of being answerable.

The framework: the Adoption Ledger

The Adoption Ledger defines “adopted” as a small set of observable behaviors per role, and tracks them like delivery scope. Each entry carries five fields.

FieldThe question it answers
RoleWhose behavior must change? Named roles, not “the business.”
Target behaviorWhat does this role do differently, stated observably?
Evidence of adoptionWhat visible fact shows the behavior is real, not reported?
OwnerWho is accountable for this role reaching the behavior?
Review dateWhen is the evidence next examined?

A worked excerpt, for one role in the program above:

FieldEntry
RoleRegional sales manager
Target behaviorApproves discounts in the workflow, within tolerance, without offline routing.
Evidence of adoptionApproval cycle time from workflow data; zero email-approval reconstructions in the region’s audit sample.
OwnerRegional sales director
Review dateDays 30, 60, and 90 after cutover

Notice what the ledger refuses to count: logins, training completion, and sentiment. Notice what it insists on: a named role, a behavior someone could observe, evidence the system already produces, an accountable owner from the business rather than the program, and a date on which the claim is re-examined. Readers of the flagship series will recognize the design language: like the RDR Model, it is a ledger of owned, revisitable commitments, because that is the shape that survives contact with an enterprise.

Why this framework is different

Traditional change management measures activities: training completed, communications sent, meetings held, champions recruited. The Adoption Ledger measures observable business behaviors, the visible proof that people are actually working differently.

The difference is the question each answers. Traditional practice asks whether the change activities happened, and every one of them can be checked off while the old process survives intact. The ledger asks whether the organization has genuinely adopted the new way of working, role by role, with evidence, and that question cannot be answered yes while the transformation fails.

The deeper shift is in what counts as success. Traditional practice ends at project completion; the ledger ends at measurable business adoption. An executive should adopt it because that is the only finish line the benefits case recognizes, and the ledger says at day 30, not month 9, exactly where the transformation is not landing and who owns fixing it.

Applying it in an enterprise program

Keep the ledger small. Five to ten roles, one to three behaviors each, chosen because the benefits case depends on them. A ledger that tracks everything is a survey with a table format.

Write behaviors observably. “Uses the new system” is not a behavior; “prices within the configured tolerance without exporting to the spreadsheet” is. If two people could disagree about whether it is happening, sharpen it.

Use evidence the systems already produce: workflow timestamps, exception volumes, audit samples, the disappearance of the artifacts the old process needed. Adoption evidence is mostly free; it just has to be looked at.

Assign owners from the business, not the program. A regional director who owns her managers’ target behavior will move it; a change lead who owns everyone’s will report on it.

And extend the ledger past demobilization. Reviews at 30, 60, and 90 days, reported through the same honest mechanics as any delivery claim; the ledger’s entries are exactly the kind of evidence-backed status that The Status Report That Tells the Truth argues leadership should be consuming. Where a behavior is not landing, that is not a communications failure to be messaged over; it is information, usually about the process design, the incentive, or the workload underneath.

Where it works, and where it does not

The Adoption Ledger earns its keep where benefits depend on many people changing how they work: ERP and CRM programs, operating-model changes, shared-services transitions, any transformation whose business case says “the process will change.” It is disproportionate for small tool rollouts with obvious value and short feedback loops, where usage is adoption and a ledger is ceremony. Its honest cost is that it removes a comfort: the program can no longer declare adoption by activity, and owners in the business must accept accountability the change team used to absorb. Some organizations find that friction unwelcome. It is also the friction working: the ledger surfaces, early and specifically, a truth that would otherwise arrive late and generally, in the benefits review, when nothing can be done about it.

Key takeaways

  • Cutover is the technical event; adoption is the business event, and only the second pays for the program.
  • Input metrics (training, communications, sentiment) can all be green while the old process survives.
  • The Adoption Ledger defines “adopted” as observable behaviors per role, each with evidence, an accountable business owner, and a review date.
  • Keep it small: the five to ten roles the benefits case actually depends on.
  • Review at 30, 60, and 90 days, past program demobilization, and treat non-adoption as information about design and incentives, not a messaging problem.
  • The payoff is transformations that hold after the program team leaves.

Where to go next

The Adoption Ledger becomes practice through the adoption progression. The natural next step is the adoption plan template, which turns the ledger into the working artifact a program runs, followed by the adoption readiness checklist, which tests before cutover whether the behaviors, owners, and evidence are actually in place, and the stakeholder alignment playbook, which builds the coalition those owners will need. The stakeholder power-and-posture map deepens the same capability as it publishes. For the reporting discipline the ledger feeds, read The Status Report That Tells the Truth, and for the decision discipline underneath every target behavior, start where the series starts: Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents.
