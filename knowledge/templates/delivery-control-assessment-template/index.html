Delivery Control Assessment Template

A one-page sheet for running the Control Sufficiency Test across a control portfolio, recording the verdict and the action for each control.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Aug 6, 2026 5 min read Foundational Project & Delivery Management #governance #delivery #assurance

Control decisions are made during governance reviews and forgotten, allowing unnecessary controls to continue indefinitely.

XLSX

What this is

A single sheet for assessing an entire control portfolio in one pass. One row per control, one verdict each, one action each.

It is a portfolio assessment, not a per-control checklist. The question is not whether a given control operates correctly; it is whether the set of controls produces the decisions leadership has to make. Individual controls can each function as designed while the portfolio delivers nothing decidable.

It is not a governance checklist and not an audit. Nothing here verifies compliance or evidence quality. It records which controls earn their place, and what changes for the ones that do not.

The reasoning behind it is set out in Delivery Controls That Earn Their Place. This sheet is the artifact for applying it.

When to use it

Run it at a scheduled governance review, where the people who own the controls are present and the findings can be acted on rather than noted. It works equally well ahead of an executive steering meeting, where the output is a shorter agenda rather than a longer one.

It earns most in two situations. The first is transformation recovery, where a programme has accumulated controls in response to incidents and nobody has stepped back. The second is an inherited control environment, where a new director needs to understand what exists and why before changing anything.

Do not run it at mobilisation. Before delivery has shape, the decisions leadership will face are not yet knowable, and assessing controls against unknown decisions produces confident answers to unanswerable questions.

Do not run it on a programme whose control set was deliberately designed and remains small. The assessment will confirm what the design intended and consume a governance slot doing it.

The assessment sheet

One row per control. Complete every column; a blank column is an unfinished assessment, not a neutral answer.

ColumnYour entry
ControlThe mechanism, named as the programme refers to it. Weekly status report, stage gate, RAID log.
Decision enabledThe decision this control informs, and the person who makes it. Both named. Visibility is not a decision.
EvidenceWhat supports the verdict, not what the control does. Duplicated another control; executive decision required; regulatory obligation; audit finding; manual workaround.
Finding that would change itA concrete finding that would alter the decision. “None” is a legitimate and common answer.
OwnerThe person who acts on a finding. A named person, not a forum and not a role nobody holds.
Review windowHow long between a finding arising and action being possible. Weeks matter; “at the next review” is often the real answer.
VerdictDecision-grade, latent, monitoring, or administration.
ActionKeep, retire, empower, or rename. One per row.

The evidence column is what makes the sheet defensible three months later. A verdict of administration supported by “duplicated the change log” survives challenge. The same verdict with an empty evidence column is an opinion someone will reverse.

How to fill it in

Start with the decision, not the control. Naming the control first invites a description of what it does, which is always available and never the point. Naming the decision first exposes the controls that inform none.

Then work across in order.

Decision. What choice does this inform, and who makes it? If the answer takes more than a sentence, the control probably serves several purposes badly.

Evidence. What supports the verdict you are about to give? Reach for the specific: this duplicated the change log; this exists because the regulator requires it; this raised a finding last quarter that nobody could action.

Finding. What would this control have to surface to change the decision? If nothing realistic would, the verdict is monitoring, and that is an honest answer rather than a failing one.

Verdict. Follow the three answers. Decision-grade where all three hold. Latent where the decision and finding are real but nobody can act in time. Monitoring where no finding would change anything. Administration where no decision is served at all.

Action. Keep, retire, empower, or rename. Empower is the one most often needed and least often chosen: a latent control usually needs an owner with authority, not removal.

There is no score, no weighting, and no maturity level. A number would let two people record agreement they do not have, and a traffic light would let a control be amber for a year.

Where this fits

Four assets divide the work, and keeping them separate is what stops any of them becoming a governance manual.

This template records. It captures the verdict and the action for each control, so a decision taken in a governance review survives the meeting it was taken in.

Delivery Controls That Earn Their Place explains. It sets out why most controls are monitoring, what the three questions are, and why a portfolio can be fully controlled and still produce nothing decidable.

The Enterprise Project Delivery Playbook governs. Its control set is deliberately small and already decision-mapped, which is the state this assessment moves a portfolio toward.

Two adjacent disciplines apply once controls have earned their place. Evidence-Backed Status governs what a control’s reporting owes the reader. The Requirements Decision Record is the form a significant decision takes once made, including the decisions this assessment produces about the controls themselves.
