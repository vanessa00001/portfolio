The ERP and CRM Platform Landscape

Platform categories compared by the constraints they impose rather than the features they demo, an orientation map for ERP and CRM decisions.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 5 min read Intermediate Enterprise Systems Strategy #ERP #CRM #platform selection #enterprise systems #reference

How to read this

Landscape guides usually compare products, which is why they expire. Feature parity moves quarterly, vendors reposition, and a document written on last year’s matrix becomes actively misleading while still looking authoritative.

This one maps categories by the constraints they impose, because constraints are the property that survives. A platform choice is the adoption of a constraint set the organization lives inside for a decade, and the classes of constraint, integration, data, upgrade, talent, and vendor, are stable even as individual products change. That argument is made in full in The Platform Decision That Constrains the Next Decade; this resource is its reference companion.

It orients, it does not recommend. No products are named, no rankings offered, no pricing quoted. Anyone claiming to rank enterprise platforms without knowing your constraint tolerance is selling something.

The three axes that matter

Categories separate along three axes, and most real decisions are a position on all three rather than a choice from a list.

Suite versus best-of-breed. One vendor covering many functions, or specialists integrated together. The trade is coherence against fit: suites reduce integration surface and concentrate vendor risk; best-of-breed maximizes functional fit and pays for it in boundaries you own forever.

Cloud-native versus migrated. Products architected for multi-tenant cloud, or established products moved there. The trade is currency against continuity: cloud-native platforms upgrade continuously and constrain customization tightly; migrated platforms carry deeper functional heritage and often bring their on-premise extension model with them.

Horizontal versus vertical. Broad platforms configured to your industry, or products built for it. The trade is flexibility against fit-out cost: horizontal platforms need more configuration and adapt to unusual models; vertical platforms arrive closer to your process and constrain you to the industry’s assumed one.

Reading the constraint classes

Integration is the constraint most underestimated at selection, because it presents as a technical detail and behaves as a permanent operating cost. Ask what the boundary looks like when you acquire a company, not when you demo.

Data determines whether you can leave, and on what terms. Extraction being technically possible is not the same as your data being usable elsewhere; the model’s shape is what you inherit.

Upgrade is where customization decisions come due, usually years after the person who made them has moved on. Continuous-upgrade platforms are unforgiving of against-the-grain work by design, which is a feature if you accept it and a trap if you fight it.

Talent is the constraint that quietly prices everything else. A thin market for a stack does not stop you; it just means every change costs more than the plan assumed, forever.

Vendor covers roadmap direction, acquisition risk, and your renewal leverage. Concentration is not automatically worse than distribution; it is a different failure mode, and the question is which one your organization can absorb.

Decision aid

Guidance on organizational constraints, not product recommendations. Read each as a tendency worth testing against your own tolerance.

Lean toward a suite when your integration capability is thin and likely to stay thin; when coherence across functions matters more than best fit in any one; when you would rather concentrate vendor risk than manage several roadmaps; or when the organization’s appetite for owning boundaries is honestly low.

Lean toward best-of-breed when one or two functions are genuinely differentiating and generic support would cost you competitively; when you already run integration as a governed capability; when regional or divisional variation makes a single model implausible; or when distributed vendor risk is preferable to concentrated dependency.

Lean toward cloud-native when you can accept the upgrade cadence and constrain customization to supported extension points, and treat that constraint as a discipline rather than a limitation.

Lean toward vertical when your industry’s process is genuinely distinctive and regulatory, and you can accept a smaller vendor and a scarcer talent market as the price of fit.

None of these are close calls in the abstract; they become close only when your constraint tolerance is unexamined.

Using this in a selection

Sequence matters. Establish your constraint tolerance before you look at any product: what your strategy implies about acquisitions, regional variation, regulatory exposure, and change appetite over ten years. Then eliminate categories whose constraint profile you cannot live with. Only then does feature comparison earn its place, on the survivors.

The categories above are the eliminating instrument, not the deciding one. Two organizations with identical requirements should reach different answers here, because their constraint tolerance differs, and a selection method that cannot produce that outcome is scoring demos rather than making a decision.

The constraint you did not price

Organizations rarely struggle because they selected the wrong software. They struggle because they underestimated the constraints they agreed to live with, and discovered the price years later, in the integration that was never simple, the upgrade that became a programme, or the specialists they could not hire. The categories in this map are not better or worse than one another. They are different bills, presented on different schedules, and the only serious question at selection is which one your organization can pay without resentment for a decade.
