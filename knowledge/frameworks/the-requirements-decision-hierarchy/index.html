The requirements decision hierarchy

Which requirements need a full decision record, which need traced rationale, and which need neither. A screen based on consequence and reversal.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Aug 6, 2026 9 min read Intermediate Business Analysis & Requirements #requirements #decision records #traceability

The question “significant” never answers

Programs that adopt decision records fail in two directions, and both failures trace to the same missing sentence.

The first is recording everything. Every requirement acquires a decision record, the register grows past the point anyone reads it, and reviewers begin skimming for the ones that look important. Within a quarter the discipline has inverted: the record exists, nobody consults it, and the effort spent producing it has bought nothing.

The second is recording nothing. No requirement is ever quite significant enough, the reasoning stays in the heads of the people who did the analysis, and three months later the program re-argues a choice it already made. This is the failure the RDR Model was built to prevent, arriving by a different route.

Both come from the same place. The guidance says significant requirements earn a decision record, and then leaves significant undefined. Absent a stated basis, significant resolves to whoever argues hardest in the room, or to whoever wrote the requirement, or to the size of the requirement itself, which is the least useful proxy available.

This framework supplies the missing sentence. The Requirement Treatment Hierarchy is a screen, not a scoring model, and it is deliberately small: two questions, four combinations, three treatments.

Where the hierarchy earns its keep

Apply the screen during analysis, once a requirement is understood well enough to reason about what happens if it is wrong. That is later than intake and earlier than baseline.

It is worth running when a program has more requirements than it can treat uniformly, which is nearly always, and when the cost of that misallocation is visible: analysts spending equal effort on a segment structure that will constrain reporting for a decade and on a page layout that changes on request.

It is not worth running on a handful of requirements. Screening twelve items produces twelve judgments and no leverage. The value appears when the population is large enough that treating it evenly is demonstrably wrong.

Two questions, four combinations

The screen asks two questions about a requirement, and neither is about how big it is.

Consequence of being wrong. If this requirement is wrong, what does it cost to discover and to live with? A wrong tax treatment surfaces at audit. A wrong field label surfaces immediately and costs a comment.

Difficulty of reversal. Once this is built and in use, what does changing it cost? A threshold in configuration is one answer. A data model that a year of transactions has been loaded against is a different one.

Answer both as high or low. Four combinations follow, and they map to three treatments.

ConsequenceDifficulty of reversalTreatment
HighHighFull decision record
HighLowTraced rationale
LowHighTraced rationale
LowLowStated requirement

The two mixed cases reach the same treatment for opposite reasons, and the reasoning matters more than the rule.

When consequence is high and reversal is easy, the cost of being wrong is real but recoverable. You capture the reasoning so the next person understands the trade that was made, and you skip the apparatus because the situation does not warrant it. The record exists to inform a future change, not to prevent one.

When consequence is low and reversal is hard, no single instance hurts much, but you are going to live with the choice. You capture the reasoning because the constraint outlives the requirement that introduced it, and because the person who inherits it will otherwise have no idea why the system behaves as it does.

Same treatment, different justification. A team that learns only the rule will apply it correctly and explain it badly, which is enough until someone challenges a classification.

The three treatments

Full decision record. Both dimensions high. The complete Requirements Decision Record: decision, rationale, owner, assumptions, revisit trigger. The revisit trigger is the field most often dropped and the one that makes the record useful later, because it states in advance what would make the decision wrong.

Traced rationale. Either dimension high, not both. The requirement, a short rationale, a named owner, and the dependency or assumption that mattered. Two or three sentences alongside the requirement, not a separate artifact.

This is deliberately not a complete RDR. There is no revisit trigger and no assumption register. If traced rationale is being written to the full RDR structure, the screen has stopped doing its job and the register will grow past the point of use.

Stated requirement. Both dimensions low. The requirement stands as written under normal traceability: it is still specified, still owned, still linked to its source and its acceptance criteria, and still subject to review. What it does not carry is decision treatment on top of that.

Stated is not undocumented. Every requirement in a managed program has an owner and a trace. The screen decides what is added, never what is removed.

Why this is not a scoring model

The obvious next step is to make the two questions numeric: score each from one to five, weight them, set a threshold. Resist it. Three things go wrong.

False precision. A requirement scoring 6.4 against a threshold of 6.0 has not been measured. A judgment has been made and then dressed as arithmetic, and the dress makes it harder to question.

Gaming. Once a threshold exists, requirements are scored toward the treatment their author wants. The rubric stops being an assessment and becomes a negotiation surface, which is worse than no rubric because it carries the appearance of objectivity.

Hidden disagreement. Two analysts scoring a requirement at six may have reached six for entirely different reasons, and the number conceals that. Two analysts answering high consequence, low reversal difficulty have said something specific enough for a third person to contest.

The screen is useful because it surfaces judgment, not because it calculates. When two people disagree about a classification, the disagreement is the valuable output: it is usually about consequence, and it is usually worth ten minutes.

Running the screen

Take the requirement as analyzed, not as first stated. A requirement that reads as a preference at intake often turns out to constrain something once its dependencies are understood, and the screen depends on that understanding.

Ask the consequence question first, and answer it in terms of what the organization experiences, not what the project experiences. A missed sprint is a project cost. A restatement to the audit committee is an organizational one.

Ask the reversal question second, and answer it about the state after go-live, not during build. Nearly everything is cheap to change before data exists. The question is what it costs afterwards.

Where the two answers disagree and the classification is contested, treat reversal difficulty as the tiebreaker for which conversation to have first. A requirement that is wrong but cheap to change is recoverable; a requirement that is locked in is where programs lose years. That is the same logic the Constraint Horizon applies to platform selection, operating one level down.

Record the answers, not just the outcome. A treatment tier with no stated basis is an assertion, and the first person to challenge it will win.

A realistic example

The following is illustrative. It is constructed to demonstrate the four combinations, not drawn from a specific engagement, and no outcomes are claimed.

Consider an ERP finance implementation with four requirements in the same release.

Chart of accounts segment structure. One sentence to state. Getting it wrong means the reporting the business asked for cannot be produced without a restructure, and restructuring a chart of accounts after a year of postings is a migration with a reconciliation problem attached. High consequence, high difficulty of reversal. Full decision record.

Approval threshold values. Set too low and finance drowns in approvals; set too high and control weakens. The consequence is real. Changing them is a configuration edit and a communication. High consequence, low difficulty of reversal. Traced rationale — capture why these numbers, who owns them, and what assumption about transaction volume they rest on.

Vendor master deduplication rule. Any individual duplicate is a nuisance. But the rule determines how the vendor master is loaded, and once it has been loaded under one rule, changing it means reprocessing the master and everything keyed to it. Low consequence, high difficulty of reversal. Traced rationale — capture the rule and the dependency, because whoever inherits the data will otherwise not know why it looks the way it does.

Invoice PDF layout. Three pages of specification: fields, placement, conditional blocks, localization. Getting it wrong produces a complaint and a change request. Low consequence, low difficulty of reversal. Stated requirement.

Note what the example does not depend on. The chart of accounts requirement is a single sentence and receives the fullest treatment. The invoice layout is the longest requirement in the release and receives none. Effort, word count, and the seniority of whoever raised it did not enter the screen at any point.

Where the hierarchy misleads

The screen allocates discretionary effort. Several classes of requirement are not discretionary, and applying it to them produces a confident wrong answer.

Statutory and regulatory requirements sit outside it. Where evidence is mandated, it is produced regardless of how the requirement screens. A low-consequence, easily-reversed control that a regulator expects documented is documented. The screen governs what a program chooses to record, not what it is obliged to.

Emergency decisions are made first and recorded after. A production incident does not wait for a classification. Act, then record retrospectively at the tier the decision would have warranted. The retrospective record is not optional, and programs that treat urgency as a permanent exemption accumulate exactly the undocumented decisions the practice exists to prevent.

Portfolio and architecture decisions belong to stronger processes. Platform selection, integration posture, and estate-level standards are governed above the requirement layer. The hierarchy operates on requirements, not on the decisions that constrain them, and using it to classify an architecture choice will understate what that choice deserves.

Requirements whose consequence cannot yet be estimated are not screened. Early discovery often cannot answer the reversal question honestly, because the solution shape is not yet known. Screening anyway produces a confident answer to an unanswerable question. Defer it; the screen belongs at analysis for exactly this reason.

Using the hierarchy to avoid documentation is a misuse, and the most likely one. Stated requirement is a treatment, not an exemption. A program in which almost everything screens low on both dimensions has not discovered efficiency; it has stopped asking what its choices cost. If the distribution looks like that, the screen is being applied to justify a decision already made.

How this fits the wider practice

The Enterprise Requirements Management Playbook runs requirements as a managed asset, and its Decide stage instructs that significant requirements receive an RDR. The Requirement Treatment Hierarchy supplies the classification that instruction assumes, so the Decide stage has a stated basis rather than a judgment call.

The RDR Model establishes that requirements carry decisions and that decisions need owners, assumptions, and revisit triggers. It does not say which requirements qualify. The Requirement Treatment Hierarchy develops that unresolved question into a screen, and leaves the record structure exactly as the model defines it.

Readers of the wider library will recognize the shape. Proportionate Assurance scales verification to consequence; this scales decision treatment the same way. The logic is shared deliberately, but the objects differ: assurance governs how much verification a piece of work earns, and this governs how much decision treatment a requirement earns. Neither substitutes for the other.
