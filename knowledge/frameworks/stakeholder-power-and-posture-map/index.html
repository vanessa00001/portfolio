The Stakeholder Power-and-Posture Map

Power without support sinks programs. The Power-and-Posture Map diagnoses six stakeholder stances and prescribes the move each one needs.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 6 min read Intermediate Change Adoption & Stakeholder Management #stakeholder management #change adoption #engagement planning #transformation #frameworks

What this framework is for

Most engagement plans are distribution lists with ambitions. Stakeholders are enumerated, a communications cadence is agreed, and everyone receives roughly the same update at roughly the same frequency. Effort spreads evenly across a population whose influence is anything but even, and the one person who can quietly stop the program receives the same newsletter as the enthusiast who has already booked training.

The Power-and-Posture Map replaces the list with a diagnosis. It sorts stakeholders on the two properties that determine what will actually happen, then prescribes a different move for each position. It is deliberately small: two axes, six stances, one cadence rule.

Why this framework exists

Traditional stakeholder matrices classify people: they sort a population into quadrants and produce a communications schedule, answering the question “how often should each group hear from us?” The Power-and-Posture Map answers a different and more consequential question: where should leadership spend its limited attention this fortnight, and on whom? It is built to drive decisions rather than distribution, because the scarce resource on a transformation is not communication frequency, it is the hours of senior attention available for the three conversations that will actually determine whether the change lands.

The two axes

Power is influence over the outcome, not seniority. It is the ability to grant or withhold something the program needs: a decision, a budget, access to people, adoption in a business unit, or a veto exercised quietly. A regional supervisor whose team can refuse to change a process holds more power over adoption than a title three levels above them.

Posture is the stakeholder’s current disposition toward the change: supportive, neutral, or resistant. Posture is a state, not a personality. It moves, and moving it is most of the work.

The rule that makes the map honest: postures are diagnosed from behavior, not reputation. What someone said in a steering meeting is weak evidence; what they funded, attended, delegated, delayed, or quietly worked around is strong evidence. A stakeholder recorded as supportive because they are agreeable in meetings is the most common error on any engagement plan, and the most expensive.

Resistant, low powerMonitorDo not argue. Chase the source of the resistance; it usually points at something real upstream.

Two positions carry most of the value. Critical is where programs are won or lost, and the instinct to defer that conversation until the case is stronger is exactly backwards: the case gets stronger by having it. Priority is where the most leverage is available, because a neutral stakeholder with power is a decision waiting for evidence, and evidence is something you can produce.

Diagnosing posture

Three sources, in ascending order of reliability.

What they say is the weakest, though not worthless: language reveals concerns even when it misrepresents commitment. What they do is stronger: attendance, delegation, funding, the speed of their decisions, whether their team’s workarounds have stopped. What they protect is strongest: every resistant stakeholder is defending something specific, a headcount, a metric they are measured on, a process their reputation rests on, or a risk they have seen realized before. Name that thing and the posture becomes tractable; miss it and you are managing a mood.

This is why resistance is treated here as information rather than obstruction. The resistant stakeholder with power frequently knows something about the current state that the supportive one does not, which makes the Critical conversation a discovery activity as much as a persuasion one.

Running the map

Map at the start, before the engagement plan is written. The map produces the plan; the plan does not produce the map.

Re-map every two weeks, or after any event that moves the ground: a leadership change, a budget decision, a public failure, a go-live. Posture moves constantly, and a map older than a fortnight is a historical document being used as a navigation chart.

Track movement, not just position. A stakeholder moving from Critical toward Priority is the signal that engagement is working, and it arrives well before adoption metrics do. A Partner drifting toward Priority is an early warning that something changed and nobody noticed.

Assign each significant stakeholder an owner on the program side who runs their move. Shared ownership of a Critical stakeholder means nobody has the difficult conversation.

Record the significant calls as decisions. Which stakeholders are Critical, who owns them, and what would change the assessment are exactly the kind of judgments that get re-argued three months later; the RDR Model discipline applies, with the revisit trigger doing the remembering.

One principle governs all of it: the map earns its place only by changing what leadership does. If the same people receive the same attention in the same rhythm after the map exists as before it, the framework has been documented rather than applied, and a documented framework has never moved a stakeholder.

Where the map is weak

It is a diagnostic, not a strategy. It tells you which move each stakeholder needs; it does not make the move, and programs that produce a beautiful map and change nothing have simply added a document.

It flattens coalitions. Stakeholders are mapped individually, but resistance is frequently collective, and two Monitors who talk to each other daily can behave like one Critical. Where you see clustering, map the cluster.

It degrades when posture is inferred from reputation, which is the failure mode to guard hardest. If the evidence for a posture is “everyone knows they are difficult,” the map is recording office politics, not diagnosing them.

And it is disproportionate for small, low-stakes change with a handful of participants, where a conversation outperforms any grid.

How this fits the wider practice

The Enterprise Discovery Playbook applies a compact version of this map during discovery, when stakeholders are first identified. This framework is the full method underneath that use: the diagnosis, the six moves, the cadence, and the evidence rules. Use the playbook to run discovery; use this to run the engagement.

Downstream, the map feeds adoption directly. The stakeholders who own target behaviors in the Adoption Ledger are usually the Partners and Priorities on this map, and the roles whose behaviors will not land are usually the Criticals you did not convert. Where the map and the ledger disagree, the ledger is right, because it measures what people did.

The point of mapping

Transformations rarely fail because leaders gave stakeholders too little attention. They fail because attention was spent evenly, on the wrong people, while the person who could stop the program was managed by newsletter. Stakeholder work is not a communications function; it is a leadership discipline, and like any leadership discipline it is exercised through where senior time is deliberately placed. The Power-and-Posture Map exists for that single purpose: to make the allocation of attention intentional, evidenced, and revisited, rather than accidental and discovered too late.
