Audit-Readiness Checklist

Audit-readiness should be a byproduct, not a project. Verify decisions, assurance tiers, evidence, lifecycle, and access before someone asks.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 3 min read Foundational Governance, Risk & Assurance #audit readiness #governance #assurance #evidence #compliance

How to use this

  • Typical completion time: 30 to 60 minutes.
  • Primary audience: Programme and project managers, PMO leads, governance leads, internal audit, delivery managers.
  • When to run it: Quarterly governance reviews, and pre-audit readiness assessments roughly four weeks ahead of a known audit.

It takes under an hour if governance has been working, and considerably longer if it has not, which is itself the finding.

Answer every item with evidence, not assent. “Yes” means you can produce the artifact within a working day. Anything else is a “no,” and every “no” becomes an owned action with a date, not a silence. The point is not to score well; it is to discover the gaps while they are still cheap to close.

Decision trail

  • Significant decisions from the last period have recorded rationale, not just outcomes.
  • Each recorded decision names an accountable owner who is still in post.
  • Assumptions behind significant decisions are stated, and their revisit triggers are live.
  • Decisions that were revisited show what changed and why.
  • Scope, definitional, and exclusion rulings are recorded where they were made, not reconstructed afterward.

If these fail, the rest of the checklist will pass on paperwork and fail under questioning: a control without a recorded reason is difficult to defend as a control at all.

Assurance and approvals

  • Every approval was made at a tier proportionate to its consequence and reversibility.
  • Tier assignments themselves are recorded, so the choice of scrutiny is defensible.
  • Independently verified items were genuinely tested, not merely read and signed.
  • Approvers were independent of the work where the tier required it.
  • Conditional approvals show their conditions, owners, and closure.

Watch for optimistic tiering: consequence argued downward to reach a cheaper approval. It is the predictable failure mode, and auditors find it quickly because the pattern is visible across a period.

Evidence

  • Evidence for controls was produced by the work, not assembled for the audit.
  • Evidence is retrievable by someone other than its author.
  • Retention matches the obligation, and expired evidence is actually gone.
  • Evidence is legible without a walkthrough: dated, attributed, and self-explaining.
  • Where evidence is a system export, the export is reproducible.

The test is simple. If preparing for an audit requires a project, the regime is producing compliance theater rather than assurance.

Data lifecycle

  • Critical data entities have named owners who would recognize the title.
  • Quality rules exist for the entities that decisions depend on, and are measured on schedule.
  • Retention terms are applied in practice, not just documented in policy.
  • Disposal actually deletes across copies and backups, and can be demonstrated.
  • Data crossing boundaries honors its declared system of record.

Access and change

  • Access is least-privilege, and the last review is recent enough to be meaningful.
  • Leavers and movers were removed or adjusted promptly, with evidence.
  • Privileged access is justified per holder, not per team.
  • Changes were assessed for impact and approved at the right tier before release.
  • Emergency changes were retro-approved within the stated window, and the pattern is not routine.

Reading the result

Count the “no” answers, then look at where they cluster rather than how many there are.

Failures concentrated in the decision trail mean governance is happening informally: the work is probably sound, the defensibility is not. Failures in evidence usually mean the controls run but nobody instrumented them, which is the cheapest gap to close. Failures in access and change are the most urgent, because they are the findings that escalate. Failures spread evenly across all sections are not an audit problem; they are a signal that governance was never operated as a capability, and the fix is the operating model, not the checklist.

Where this comes from

The discipline behind these checks is set out in Assurance Is What Lets Leaders Say Yes, which argues that assurance exists to enable a confident yes and that evidence should accumulate as a byproduct of proportionate governance. The Enterprise Data Governance Playbook covers the lifecycle and access items in depth, and the Enterprise Project Delivery Playbook covers the change and approval items as they operate on a program. For the decision-recording practice the first section depends on, see Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents and its template.
