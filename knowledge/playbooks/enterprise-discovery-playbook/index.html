Enterprise Discovery Playbook

A consultant-grade approach to enterprise discovery across processes, systems, stakeholders, and risks, so solution design starts from evidence.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 14 min read Intermediate Business Analysis & Requirements #discovery #business analysis #requirements #workshops #transformation

By the end you will have: A validated, prioritized picture of the current state - processes, systems, stakeholders, pain points, and risks - with an executive-ready readout and a decision trail, ready for solution design.

  • Time to execute4 to 8 weeks, depending on scope
  • RolesBusiness Analyst, Project Manager, Solution Architect, Transformation Lead
  • PrerequisitesNamed executive sponsor, Agreed scope boundary, Committed stakeholder access

Executive overview

Enterprise discovery is the work that happens before solution design begins: understanding how the organization actually operates, where it hurts, what its systems really do, and what its people will tell you when asked well. Programs rarely fail because discovery was skipped; they fail because discovery was performed as a ritual, a few workshops and a requirements list, and the program built confidently on what it did not know.

This playbook is the structured alternative. It runs discovery as an evidence-gathering engagement with a defined lifecycle: prepare, engage, map, assess, validate, prioritize, and read out. Followed end to end, it produces the outcome named above; used selectively, each section stands alone as a working guide. Throughout, it applies the Knowledge Center’s frameworks where they belong, because discovery is where most of them are born: decisions get recorded, claims get evidence, and findings get owners.

When to use this playbook

Use it before any consequential build, buy, or change: ERP and CRM programs, systems replacement or selection, process transformation, integration initiatives, operating-model changes, and automation portfolios. Use it when inheriting a troubled program whose foundations are suspect, because re-discovery is usually cheaper than continued guessing.

Scale it down, not out, for smaller work: a two-week discovery keeps every stage but shortens each. Skip it only when the problem is genuinely well understood, narrow, and cheap to reverse, and be honest about how rarely all three are true at enterprise scale.

Discovery principles

Six principles govern everything that follows.

  1. Evidence over assertion. What people say is data about beliefs; what the process and systems show is data about reality. Collect both, and label which is which.
  2. Current state before future state. The urge to design is constant and must be deferred. You cannot price a journey from an unknown starting point.
  3. Decisions get recorded. Every significant choice made during discovery, scope boundaries, definitions, exclusions, follows the RDR Model: decision, rationale, owner, assumptions, revisit trigger.
  4. Pain points are symptoms. The complaint is where investigation starts, not where it ends. Chase every pain to the process, system, incentive, or decision underneath it.
  5. Stakeholders are sources and audiences at once. How you engage them during discovery shapes whether they adopt what comes after; discovery is the first act of change management.
  6. Findings earn their place. Everything in the readout traces to evidence, an owner, and a consequence. If a finding changes no decision, it is trivia.

Discovery preparation checklist

Complete before the first stakeholder session. Gaps here become delays later.

  • Sponsor confirmed, with an agreed escalation path for access problems.
  • Scope boundary written and signed: which processes, entities, systems, and geographies are in, and explicitly which are out. Record the boundary as a decision with an owner and revisit trigger.
  • Discovery plan drafted: stages, sessions, participants, and dates (see Discovery planning).
  • Document harvest requested: process documentation, system inventories, org charts, prior assessments, incident and exception reports, audit findings. Request early; chase weekly.
  • Access secured: read access to key systems or exports arranged, data-handling rules confirmed.
  • Team briefed: roles assigned (facilitator, scribe, analyst), templates ready, terminology aligned.
  • Logistics locked: rooms or calls, calendars held, recording and note conventions agreed.

Stakeholder mapping

Map stakeholders before engaging them, on two questions: how much power does this person hold over the outcome, and what is their current posture toward it? Power without support is the risk; support without power is the wasted effort of many engagement plans.

Posture \ PowerHigh powerLow power
SupportivePartner: co-design with them, use them to open doorsAmplifier: equip them, they carry the message
NeutralPriority: their questions define your evidence planInform: keep them warm, low effort
ResistantCritical: engage first, one to one, listen before presentingMonitor: their resistance is data, chase its source

Three rules of use. Map postures from evidence (what people have said and done), not reputation. Revisit the map every two weeks; discovery itself moves postures. And treat resistance as information: the resistant stakeholder usually knows something about the current state that the supportive one does not.

Discovery planning

Plan discovery as a short program with its own controls. A workable default for a mid-sized scope:

WeekFocusPrimary activities
1PrepareDocument harvest, stakeholder map, session scheduling, terminology baseline
2 to 3Engage and mapInterviews, process workshops, system walkthroughs
4AssessCurrent state assembly, pain point analysis, data verification
5ValidatePlayback sessions, correction rounds, gap-closing interviews
6Prioritize and read outPrioritization workshop, deliverable assembly, executive readout

Typical duration ranges, within the four-to-eight-week envelope: preparation, 2 to 5 business days; stakeholder workshops and interviews, 1 to 3 weeks; current state assembly and assessment, 1 week; validation sessions, 1 week; prioritization and executive readout, 2 to 3 business days. Actual timelines depend on organizational size, project complexity, stakeholder availability, and regulatory requirements; scale the stages, never skip them.

Report discovery progress the way any honest program reports: as claims with evidence. “Discovery on track” is theater; “14 of 18 planned sessions complete, two workstreams thin on evidence, decision needed on warehouse access” is the Evidence-Backed Status discipline applied to discovery itself.

Workshop facilitation guide

Workshops are where discovery earns or loses its reputation. Run them tightly.

Before: publish purpose, questions, and expected outputs in the invitation; never ask a room to “discuss the process.” Cap attendance at eight to ten doers, the people who perform the work, with managers in separate sessions so the room can speak. Prepare the walls or the board: a process skeleton to react to beats a blank page.

During: open with the boundary (what this session covers and does not). Facilitate for specifics: “walk me through the last time this happened” outperforms “how does this usually work” every time. Separate the three streams as they arrive: facts (how it works), pains (what hurts), and ideas (what they wish for). Park solutions visibly and respectfully; the parking lot is a promise, not a bin. Close every session by reading back what was heard and naming what happens next.

After: circulate the structured note within forty-eight hours while memory is warm, with corrections invited by a stated date. An uncorrected note becomes evidence; an uncirculated one becomes a dispute.

Process mapping approach

Map processes at two levels and resist the third. Level one is the landscape: the end-to-end value streams in scope, one page each, showing major stages, systems touched, and handoffs. Level two is the working map: for each in-scope process, the activities, actors, systems, inputs, outputs, decision points, and exceptions, captured in the workshop and verified against reality afterward. Level three, keystroke-level detail, is for automation design later, not discovery; mapping everything at that depth is how discovery becomes archaeology.

Two disciplines matter more than notation. Map the process as it is actually performed, including the workarounds, the spreadsheet on the side, and the approval that happens by email; the official process is an input, not the answer. And mark every exception path found, with its frequency if anyone can evidence it, because exceptions are where cost, risk, and the next program’s failures live. Where automation is in scope, tag each mapped activity provisionally against the Toil-Judgment Line: rule-bound and verifiable, or ambiguous and accountable. The tags are hypotheses for later, not commitments.

Current state assessment

Assemble the evidence into a current state assessment across four layers, kept deliberately parallel so findings can be traced across them.

Process: the maps above, with volumes, cycle times, and exception rates where evidence exists. Systems: the inventory of applications in scope, what each actually does (versus what it was bought to do), integration methods, data flows, known fragilities, and where spreadsheets have become systems of record. People: roles, capacity consumed by in-scope work, key-person dependencies, and where knowledge lives only in heads. Data: where the truth for each critical entity lives, where it is duplicated, and where reconciliation effort is spent.

The standing rule: every statement in the assessment carries its source, a session, a document, a system export, or an observation. Assertions without sources go to a follow-up list, not into the assessment.

Pain point identification

Collect pains everywhere, but process them with discipline: a raw pain list is a complaint log; a processed one is an asset. For each pain, capture what hurts, who it hurts, how often, what it costs (in time, money, risk, or morale, evidenced where possible), and, critically, its proximate cause in the current state: which process step, system behavior, data gap, or decision produces it.

Then cluster upward. Individual pains usually resolve into a much smaller set of root themes, a broken handoff, a system boundary, a missing decision owner, and the themes, not the individual complaints, are what the opportunity assessment works on. Expect the map of pains to redraw the stakeholder map: where pain concentrates, posture follows.

Opportunity assessment

Translate pain themes into opportunity statements: what could be true instead, and what it would take. Keep them honest by writing each with four parts: the theme it addresses, the change it proposes (process, system, data, or organizational, most real opportunities mix them), the benefit logic (which cost or risk it removes, traced to the pain evidence), and its rough scale of effort.

Resist two temptations. Do not design solutions; an opportunity says “eliminate the reconciliation between systems A and B,” not “implement middleware X.” And do not let opportunities exceed their evidence: an opportunity built on one workshop anecdote is a hypothesis and is labeled as one.

Requirements capture

Discovery produces early requirements, and how they are captured determines whether they survive. Capture them as statements of need bound to their source and context: who needs it, for which process, evidenced by what, and why it matters. Resist premature precision; discovery-stage requirements are directional, and false exactness now becomes false authority later.

For the significant ones, the contested, expensive, or irreversible, apply the RDR Model from the start: the decision inside the requirement, its rationale, its owner, its assumptions, its revisit trigger. The Requirements Decision Record template is the working artifact. Requirements that begin life as owned decisions do not need to be retrofitted with reasoning when solution design starts asking why.

Decision log

Discovery makes decisions constantly, scope calls, definitional rulings, exclusions, method choices, and unlogged decisions get re-argued for months. Keep one decision log for the engagement, in RDR form, from day one. Log at minimum: the scope boundary and every change to it, definitional rulings (what counts as an order, a customer, an exception), inclusion and exclusion calls, and any finding a stakeholder disputed and how it was resolved. The log is a discovery deliverable in its own right, and often the one the program refers back to longest.

Risk register

Discovery surfaces risk in two directions and the register holds both. Risks to discovery: stakeholder access failing, evidence unavailable, scope pressure, key sessions slipping. Risks discovered: key-person dependencies, fragile integrations, compliance exposures, data quality that would undermine any future build. Record each with its evidence, its owner, and its consequence, and grade the discovered risks by consequence and reversibility, the same two properties the Proportionate Assurance model uses, so the program that follows inherits a register already shaped for proportionate scrutiny.

Validation sessions

Unvalidated discovery is opinion with formatting. Before anything is prioritized, play the findings back to the people who provided them: the process maps to the doers who described them, the pain themes to the people who feel them, the systems picture to the people who run them.

Run playback as correction, not presentation: “here is what we heard; where is it wrong?” Expect and welcome corrections, and log material ones as decisions. Where two credible sources conflict, do not average them; investigate, because the conflict is usually pointing at an exception path or a regional variation worth knowing. Validation is also the first adoption act: stakeholders who see their corrections land begin to own the findings, which is the seed the Adoption Ledger will later grow.

Prioritization framework

Prioritize validated opportunities on two axes: business consequence (the evidenced cost of leaving the pain in place) and tractability (how feasibly the organization can act, considering effort, dependency, and readiness). Four quadrants follow, and each has a verb.

High consequenceLow consequence
High tractabilityAct: the program’s first candidatesBatch: cheap wins, bundle them
Low tractabilityPlan: real, hard, needs sequencing and sponsorshipDefer: log and revisit

Run prioritization as a workshop with the sponsor and the owning stakeholders, scoring from the evidence base, and record the resulting sequence as a decision, owner and revisit trigger included, because priorities set in a workshop and never revisited become the next program’s inherited dogma.

Discovery deliverables

The engagement produces six artifacts, and their standard is the same as this library’s: usable, not ornamental.

  1. Current state assessment: the four-layer picture, every statement sourced.
  2. Process map pack: landscape and working maps, exceptions marked.
  3. Pain and opportunity register: themes, evidence, and opportunity statements.
  4. Decision log: every significant call, in RDR form.
  5. Risk register: both directions, graded for the program to inherit.
  6. Executive readout: the findings that change decisions, next section.

Executive readout

The readout is not a summary of effort; it is the findings that change decisions, presented so leaders can act. A structure that works: the answer first (the three to five findings that matter most, each with its evidence and its consequence); the current state in one honest page; the prioritized opportunities with their logic; the risks leadership must own; and the recommended next step with what it requires.

A standard presentation structure that serves this purpose: Executive Summary (the answer, first); Current-State Assessment (one honest page); Key Pain Points (themes, with evidence); Strategic Opportunities (with their benefit logic); Decisions Required (explicit, owned, dated); Risks (the ones leadership must own); Recommended Next Steps; and Delivery Roadmap (the sequence the prioritization produced). The purpose of the readout is to enable executive decision-making, not to summarize workshop findings; every section exists to move a decision, and any slide that does not is cut.

Hold the readout to the Evidence-Backed Status standard: every claim carries its evidence, and every section ends with the decision requested, even when the request is “no decision today; this is the dependency to watch.” A readout that informs but never asks has wasted the room.

Common mistakes

  • Designing during discovery: the future state conversation drags every session off the current state. Park it, visibly.
  • Interviewing only managers: the process lives with the doers; managers describe the process as designed, doers describe it as performed.
  • Treating documentation as truth: the official process map is a historical artifact until verified.
  • Averaging conflicting accounts instead of investigating the conflict.
  • Collecting pains without causes, producing a complaint log no one can act on.
  • Skipping validation to save a week, and spending a quarter defending disputed findings.
  • Boiling the ocean: level-three mapping everywhere, evidence perfectionism, discovery that never ends. Time-box, and log the gaps honestly instead.
  • Losing the decisions: scope calls and rulings made in hallways, re-argued forever. The log exists for this.

Discovery maturity model

Discovery is a capability, not a project phase, and organizations can locate themselves on a five-level ladder.

LevelCharacteristics
Level 1Ad hoc interviews with inconsistent outputs; findings live in individual notebooks
Level 2Structured workshops and documented findings; quality depends on the facilitator
Level 3Evidence-based discovery using repeatable templates; findings are sourced and validated
Level 4Cross-functional validation with governance checkpoints; decisions logged and assured
Level 5Enterprise discovery capability integrated into delivery methodology; every program starts from evidence

The ladder is climbed deliberately: each engagement that follows this playbook should leave the organization one habit better, a template adopted, a decision log kept, a validation round made standard. Treat discovery capability as something to continuously improve, not as a one-time project activity that resets to zero with each new program.

Success metrics

Discovery succeeded if, four to eight weeks after it began: the readout was delivered with every finding evidenced and none successfully disputed on facts; the sponsor made the next-step decision from it without commissioning a second discovery; solution design started from the assessment rather than from scratch; the decision log is being referenced, not re-litigated; and the stakeholders who provided the evidence recognize themselves in the findings. Session counts and page counts are activity, not success; the test is whether decisions downstream got easier.

Learning path

Discovery feeds the whole library, and the deeper guides extend from here. Within this capability, the Requirements Decision Record template operationalizes the capture discipline, and the elicitation and scope-triage playbooks will deepen the session craft as they publish. Across capabilities: Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents for the decision discipline underneath everything here; The Status Report That Tells the Truth for how discovery reports and reads out; Automate the Toil, Not the Judgment for what the activity tags become; Adoption Is the Real Go-Live for where stakeholder engagement leads; Assurance Is What Lets Leaders Say Yes for the risk grading the program inherits; and The Platform Decision That Constrains the Next Decade where discovery precedes a selection.

How this playbook uses the framework library

A closing orientation for readers new to the Knowledge Center. This playbook did not invent its methods; it applies the library’s proprietary frameworks at the moments discovery needs them: the RDR Model wherever decisions are made (scope, requirements, priorities), Evidence-Backed Status for discovery’s own reporting and the executive readout, the Toil-Judgment Line for provisional automation tagging, the Adoption Ledger’s logic for validation-as-adoption, the Proportionate Assurance grading for the risk register, and the Constraint Horizon wherever discovery leads into a platform choice. That is by design: the frameworks are one system, and discovery is where an engagement meets most of them for the first time.
