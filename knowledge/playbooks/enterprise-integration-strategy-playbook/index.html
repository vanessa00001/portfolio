Enterprise Integration Strategy Playbook

A practical methodology for selecting, designing, governing, and evolving enterprise integrations across ERP, CRM, AI automation, and legacy systems.

VS Vanessa Salonoy Jul 22, 2026 12 min read Advanced Enterprise Systems Strategy #integration #enterprise architecture #APIs #event-driven #middleware

By the end you will have: A governed integration strategy - patterns chosen per boundary on criteria, decisions recorded, failure and observation designed, middleware chosen inside its constraints - that keeps the landscape operable as it grows and changes.

  • Time to execute2 to 4 weeks to establish; governs the landscape for its life
  • RolesEnterprise Architect, Solution Architect, Integration Lead, Project Manager
  • PrerequisitesA system landscape inventory (even a rough one), The solution designs or requirements creating integration demand, A named integration authority

Executive overview

Integration is the part of the enterprise nobody buys on purpose and everybody operates forever. Landscapes do not fail at their centers; they fail at their boundaries, where systems meet on patterns chosen in a hurry, on contracts nobody wrote down, with failure behavior discovered in production. And because every program adds boundaries but almost none retires them, the integration estate grows by accretion until it is the quiet majority of operational risk.

An integration strategy is the alternative to accretion. It is not a middleware purchase and not a diagram of arrows; it is a small set of governed decisions, which patterns are used when, how boundaries fail and are observed, where truth lives, and how the estate evolves, applied every time a new boundary is born. This playbook follows the Enterprise Solution Design Playbook: where that playbook designs each boundary inside a solution, this one governs the boundaries as a landscape, across solutions and across years.

When to use this playbook

Use it when the landscape is growing (an ERP or CRM program adding a wave of boundaries), when it is hurting (integration incidents are a standing agenda item), when it is consolidating (platform rationalization, post-merger), or when AI automation is arriving (new flows crossing old systems). Establish the strategy once, then apply it as standing governance: every new boundary passes through it, and the existing estate is brought under it opportunistically, worst-first, rather than through a heroic remediation program that will not survive its first budget review.

Principles

  1. Boundaries are decisions. Every integration is a recorded choice of pattern, contract, truth, and failure behavior, per the RDR Model discipline; an ADR per significant boundary is the working form.
  2. Criteria over fashion. Patterns are chosen by the boundary’s properties, never by what is current, familiar, or already licensed.
  3. Fewer patterns, well governed. A landscape that does five patterns well beats one that does twelve badly; variety is a cost you spend deliberately.
  4. Design the failure first. A boundary is not designed until what-happens-when-the-other-side-is-down is designed; hope is not a failure mode.
  5. Truth is singular. Every data element crossing a boundary has one system of record, and the boundary knows which side it is.
  6. The estate is a portfolio. Boundaries are inventoried, owned, health-tracked, and retired, the way any asset portfolio is managed.

From solution design to integration strategy

The Solution Design Playbook decides each boundary’s pattern, contract, and failure behavior inside a program. This playbook supplies what the program-level view cannot: the standards those decisions are made against, the reuse they should discover (the boundary you need often half-exists), the portfolio they join, and the governance that keeps a hundred locally-reasonable decisions from becoming a globally unreasonable landscape. Mechanically: every integration ADR produced in solution design lands in the estate inventory, is checked against the pattern standards, and inherits the observation and failure conventions, so the landscape stays one system of intent rather than an archive of exceptions.

Choosing patterns by criteria

The Solution Design Playbook compares the five approaches (API-first, event-driven, batch, file-based, hybrid) at the boundary level; the strategy’s job is to make that comparison a standard instead of a per-program debate. Publish the decision criteria as questions any architect can answer: What latency does the business genuinely need, not want? What volume and shape is the data? Which side owns truth for what crosses? What consistency can the consuming process tolerate? What is the failure blast radius? What can both systems actually support without heroics? Then map criteria to sanctioned patterns, and require an ADR wherever a boundary departs from the mapping. The point is not rigidity; it is that departure becomes a decision with an owner instead of a preference with momentum.

No single pattern is universally correct, and the matrix must never be read as a ranking. Selection is driven by the business capability the boundary serves and its operational requirements: the latency the process genuinely needs, the volume and shape of the data, and what both sides can support. A pattern that is wrong for the boundary is wrong however well it scores in general.

Integration decision records

Significant boundaries get ADRs with the standard five fields, and three integration-specific disciplines. State the contract as part of the decision: what crosses, in what shape, versioned how, because an integration without a written contract is an agreement between two teams’ memories. State the reversibility honestly: some patterns (files, batch) are cheap to change, others (event backbones, deep API coupling) are constraint adoptions in miniature, deserving the Constraint Horizon’s ten-year questions. And state the revisit trigger operationally: volume thresholds, contract version changes, or the retirement of a system on either side.

Middleware and platform choices

Middleware is where integration strategies go to become procurement, and the discipline is to resist that inversion: the strategy chooses the middleware, never the reverse. Evaluate integration platforms, ESBs, iPaaS, API management, event backbones, exactly as the Constraint Horizon prescribes for any platform: integration model, data posture, upgrade economics, talent market, and vendor trajectory, over ten years, before features. Two strategy-level truths to hold: middleware does not remove boundary decisions, it centralizes where they are enforced; and the middleware itself becomes the landscape’s largest single constraint set, which is a fine trade when made knowingly and a decade of lock-in when made by demo.

Legacy systems

Legacy is not a pattern problem; it is a constraint problem. The old system supports what it supports, files where you wanted events, nightly where you wanted now, and the strategy’s job is honest accommodation: wrap where feasible (an API or event facade over the legacy interface, so consumers see the sanctioned pattern while the legacy side sees what it can bear), stage where not (accepting the batch boundary and designing the reconciliation it implies), and quarantine the constraint so it does not propagate, the facade absorbs the legacy’s shape instead of every consumer inheriting it. Every accommodation is an ADR with a retirement trigger, because legacy accommodations outlive the legacy unless someone wrote down when to remove them.

AI automation across boundaries

AI-driven flows are integration consumers with two extra properties: they act, and they are confidently wrong at scale when fed stale or broken data. Apply the Toil-Judgment Line to what the flow does, and its Escalation Seam to the boundaries it crosses: the seam is designed into the integration, so when the boundary degrades, the automation stands down to a human with the case, its evidence, and its history, rather than acting on yesterday’s truth. Boundary health signals feed the seam’s stand-down conditions directly; an automation consuming a boundary with no health signal is a judgment delegation nobody decided to make.

Failure, observation, and contracts

Three conventions applied estate-wide, so every boundary answers them the same way. Failure: each boundary’s designed behavior when the other side is down, slow, or wrong (queue, retry with backoff, degrade, halt), with blast radius stated; failure behavior is part of the contract, not an operational discovery. Observation: every boundary emits health someone watches, flow, latency, error and reconciliation signals, because a boundary observed only by its consumers is observed by incident. Contracts: versioned, owned on both sides, with breaking changes governed like the changes they are; the Evidence-Backed Status discipline applies to estate reporting, where “integration is fine” is a claim that owes its evidence, trend, and the decision requested.

The integration readiness assessment

Run this assessment per boundary before implementation begins; it is the pre-build gate that turns strategy into practice. Each item is answered with evidence, and any “no” becomes an owned action or a recorded, accepted risk, never a silence.

  • System readiness: can both systems actually support the chosen pattern at the required volume, today, without heroics?
  • Data quality: is what will cross the boundary fit to act on, and has someone measured rather than assumed it?
  • API maturity: where the pattern needs interfaces, do they exist, are they versioned, and are they supported by their owners?
  • Security: are authentication, authorization, and data protection designed for this boundary at the tier its consequence demands?
  • Monitoring: will the boundary emit health signals from day one, and does someone own watching them?
  • Operational support: who runs this boundary at 2 a.m., and do they know it exists before it fails?
  • Ownership: are both sides of the contract owned by named roles, business and technical?
  • Change management: is there a path for versioning the contract and consuming systems when either side must change?

Used honestly, the assessment moves failure discovery from production to paper, which is the cheapest place it will ever be found.

Governance and evolution

Integration governance follows the Proportionate Assurance model: sanctioned-pattern boundaries with modest blast radius are self-attested against the standards; material boundaries get peer review; the vital few, regulated data crossings, event backbones, anything whose failure stops revenue, get independent verification. The estate evolves under the same governance: quarterly, the inventory is read for consolidation candidates (three boundaries doing one job), retirement candidates (boundaries whose systems or triggers have expired), and pattern promotions or retirements. An estate that only ever grows is not being governed; it is being accumulated.

Governance also needs named responsibilities, not just tiers and cadences. The working split:

Ownership is per boundary and named, decision rights follow the table rather than the loudest voice, and the escalation path is short and published: operator to engineer to architecture to the integration authority, with the business owner engaged wherever meaning or truth is in question. A boundary whose escalation path is discovered during its first incident did not have one.

Metrics

Measure the estate as a portfolio. Leading: percentage of boundaries with recorded decisions and written contracts, percentage emitting health signals, pattern-exception rate on new boundaries, and time-to-integrate for a standard boundary (the strategy’s speed dividend, which is the number that buys its budget). Lagging: integration incidents by boundary and cause, mean time to detect boundary failure (before or after the business felt it), and reconciliation effort trend. Report them as claims with evidence, and let the leading set predict what the lagging set will otherwise confirm.

Common mistakes

  • Buying the middleware first and calling it the strategy.
  • Pattern by fashion: event-driven because it is current, files because they are familiar.
  • The unwritten contract: two teams’ memories, versioned by incident.
  • Failure behavior discovered in production instead of designed at the boundary.
  • Point-to-point accretion: every program solving only its own boundary, forever.
  • Legacy accommodations without retirement triggers, outliving the legacy itself.
  • Observing boundaries through their consumers’ incidents.
  • An estate that only grows: no inventory, no consolidation, no retirement, no owner.

Integration maturity model

LevelNameCharacteristics
Level 1Point-to-PointAccretion without inventory; contracts unwritten; failures discovered in production
Level 2ManagedAn inventory exists; patterns named; health observed on the worst boundaries
Level 3StandardizedSanctioned pattern set with published decision criteria; ADRs on significant boundaries; contracts versioned
Level 4GovernedEstate-wide failure and observation conventions; proportionate review tiers; exceptions owned, not silent
Level 5Enterprise Integration PlatformThe estate runs as a strategic capability: consolidation and retirement are routine, time-to-integrate is a managed number, and AI flows inherit seams by construction

The evolution from tactical to strategic follows the levels in order: organizations move from integrations as project by-products (Levels 1 and 2) to integration as a standardized discipline (Level 3) to a governed estate (Level 4) and finally to integration as an enterprise capability in its own right (Level 5), where the landscape accelerates programs instead of taxing them. One habit per program, made standard.

Deliverables

  1. The estate inventory: boundaries, patterns, contracts, owners, health.
  2. The sanctioned pattern set with published decision criteria.
  3. ADRs for significant boundaries, contracts included.
  4. The estate-wide failure, observation, and contract conventions.
  5. The governance record: tiers, reviews, exceptions with owners.
  6. The evolution log: consolidations, retirements, pattern changes.

Learning path

This playbook extends the delivery sequence outward from a single solution to the landscape. Behind it: the Enterprise Solution Design Playbook for boundary-level design, the Enterprise Requirements Management Playbook for the baseline integration answers to, and the Enterprise Discovery Playbook where the landscape is first evidenced. Around it: The Platform Decision That Constrains the Next Decade for middleware and platform choices, Automate the Toil, Not the Judgment for the flows the estate increasingly carries, Assurance Is What Lets Leaders Say Yes for the governance tiers, and Requirements Are Decisions, Not Documents with its template for the record discipline every boundary carries.

How this playbook uses the framework library

The pattern, applied at landscape scale: the RDR Model’s discipline becomes the integration ADR, per boundary. The Constraint Horizon governs middleware and the deep-coupling choices that are platform decisions in disguise. The Toil-Judgment Line and Escalation Seam shape every AI flow the estate carries. Proportionate Assurance sets review tiers, and Evidence-Backed Status carries estate reporting. One system, one language, now spanning discovery, requirements, design, and the boundaries that hold the enterprise together.
